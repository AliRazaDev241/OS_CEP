                                               Abstract

          Sharing an accelerator among inference tenants can improve useful capacity, yet con-
      tention changes latency and the meaning of isolation depends on the mechanism. We review
      temporal scheduling, NVIDIA Multi-Process Service (MPS), Multi-Instance GPU (MIG),
      and API-level remoting or virtualization with single-device inference evidence as the techni-
      cal unit. A submitted protocol guides four publisher-platform searches and source-anchored
      screening. The current 43 accepted primary studies are a working set, not a completed final
      systematic selection: 45 extension reports await assessment, 96 earlier reports have unre-
      solved retrieval, and four record links await adjudication. We distinguish same-experiment
      effects from cross-paper interpretation, and separate throughput, SLO goodput, latency
      percentiles, resource separation, security and fault containment. The evidence supports
      conditional choices: temporal control for strict priority when its scheduling constraints fit;
      tuned MPS for packing compatible inference; MIG for explicit partitioning where hardware,
      profiles and memory fit; and API control when interception and data movement fit the
      latency budget. Hybrids require local validation. These recommendations must be checked
      against the completed selection and missing configuration fields before submission.

1     Introduction and engineering problem
In a multi-tenant inference server, requests from distinct models or clients compete for execution
slots, accelerator memory, caches and memory bandwidth. Exclusive GPU allocation simplifies
attribution of latency to a tenant but can leave capacity idle when individual jobs are small or
bursty. Concurrent execution can raise aggregate useful work while delaying a high-priority
request, especially when the co-tenant’s kernel or transfer pattern is unpredictable. Selecting
a sharing mechanism is therefore an operating-systems decision about admission, scheduling,
resource boundaries and observability, rather than a single throughput benchmark.
The assigned alternatives operate at different layers. Time-slicing switches or queues work over
time; MPS enables multi-process execution and configurable active-thread shares; MIG presents
hardware-defined GPU instances on supported NVIDIA devices; API remoting or virtualization
routes calls, context state or data through an additional control path. Research systems frequently
combine these with batching, model placement, kernel interception, memory management and
feedback control. Such combinations cannot be evaluated as if one toggle alone produced the
reported gain. The unit of our central comparison is concurrent inference on one physical GPU;
multi-GPU outcomes are context unless a local sharing experiment can be identified.
We ask five analytical questions. RQ1: Which sharing/control mechanisms and boundaries have
been evaluated for multi-tenant inference? RQ2: Which utilization, density, latency, fairness
and isolation outcomes are actually measured, and which are comparable? RQ3: Under what
workload, hardware and configuration conditions do results change? RQ4: What trade-offs and
contradictory evidence emerge, and what experimental differences may explain them? RQ5:
Which choice is defensible under stated requirements and evidence limits? The answers are
synthesized in Sections 5–10, rather than determined by counting papers in each category.


2     Review protocol and search execution
2.1    Protocol, eligibility and review procedure
The submitted SLR_protocol.tex/pdf is the baseline. Its Group A execution record and dated
amendments are in groups/group-a/protocol.md and evidence/protocol_amendments.
md. The literature cutoff was extended to 11 October 2026. The study must be an English-
language, peer-reviewed original full journal or conference report with relevant concurrent
inference evidence and adequate technical detail; mixed inference/training work is used only
where the inference result is separately identifiable. Training-only, cluster-only, editorial, survey
and vendor-only material does not enter the primary set. A report-version link does not create
an additional underlying study. Source retrieval failure remains an access outcome, not an
eligibility exclusion.
One AI reviewer carried out the recorded screening and quality appraisal under the user’s
instruction. This is a limitation, not a claim of independent student consensus. Stable record,
report and study IDs, reasons and extraction anchors are retained in the supporting workbook
and CSV files. Quality uses ten criteria scored 0, 0.5 or 1, yielding a total out of 10; it assesses
reporting and experimental adequacy, not proof of a mechanism’s causal superiority. A human
audit of screening, quality scoring and the remaining reports is still needed.

3     Evidence base, extraction and comparability
The working set contains 43 distinct accepted study IDs, each with a preferred report, a quality
row and a bounded extraction row. The bibliography preserves those report keys. The complete
study register in Appendix A gives the title, year, mechanism and quality score; Appendix B
maps a concise extracted observation and limitation to the source location. The workbook holds
fuller version and screening records. Peer-reviewed status of a venue does not validate all figures
or indicate that our team reran its experiments. Some source copies are Markdown conversions
with interleaved columns or inaccessible plot pixels; untranscribed values are never interpolated.
We distinguish request throughput (all processed requests per unit time) from SLO goodput
(requests meeting a specified deadline), GPU busy time, SM occupancy, instance count, energy
and a paper’s custom utilization/efficiency ratio. A lower instance count in a fleet experiment is
not a directly measured local GPU occupancy gain. Mean latency, p90/p95/p98/p99, time to
first token (TTFT), time between tokens (TBT), job completion time (JCT), and violation rates
have different meanings. A paper’s p99 under one arrival process cannot be numerically pooled
with another paper’s mean under a different model, GPU or baseline. InferFair’s unfairness
ratio is also distinct from security isolation. The evidence matrix therefore uses within-study
comparisons and explicitly marks indirect cross-study recommendations.
For an interpretable comparison one must know the GPU SKU and memory, driver/runtime,
MIG profile or MPS active-thread quota, model and input/output sizes, batching, tenant mix,
arrival trace, SLO definition, comparator resource budget, warm-up and measurement windows,
and uncertainty estimate. Some of these parameters remain NOT EXTRACTED in individual
records, so a formal effect-size meta-analysis is not justified. Direct security and injected-fault
tests are scarce; interference reduction, resource separation, adversarial confidentiality and fault
containment are separate properties.


4     Mechanism taxonomy and RQ1
4.1    Temporal admission, switching and priority
Clockwork schedules predictable GPU work with SLO-aware goodput as a central outcome
[1]. PipeSwitch explores pipelined context switches [2]. REEF, Sirius, Orion, XSched, Paella,
SPLIT, Miriam, SwitchFlow and ElasticRoom use different combinations of preemption, priority,
kernel or subgraph control, compilers and feedback [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. These are not
interchangeable implementations of a generic time slice: a scheduler that can intercept launches,
preempt selected kernels and control memory handover has a different cost and failure mode
from exclusive model execution. SwitchFlow’s concurrent inference evidence is in its inference-
specific experiment; its larger mixed inference/training p95 example is a separate stratum
[10].



                                                 6
Temporal control is attractive when a small high-priority request should interrupt or avoid
best-effort interference. It still needs admitted kernels to finish or be preempted in time, enough
memory for models and a credible worst-case workload profile. More aggressive interruption
can lower throughput of the best-effort tenant. A priority guarantee is a performance property
under evaluated arrivals, not an adversarial security boundary. The strength of this inference is
direct for the cited within-study experiments and indirect when generalized to a new GPU or
model.

4.2    MPS and software spatial quotas
MPS enables simultaneous work and configurable active-thread limits; research systems use it to
provision GPUlets, adapt quotas, right-size kernels or shape interference [12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17,
18]. Bless and DeInfer add scheduling or prediction, so their gains cannot be assigned to MPS
alone [19, 18]. A quota may limit some compute contention while cache, memory traffic and
process behavior remain shared. VastPipe’s A100 pair and separate AMD CU-mask experiments
must not be pooled; the latter is not NVIDIA MPS [14]. DeInfer directly observes remaining
latency interference under MPS spatial allocation [18].
Provisioning can improve density when models are small enough and arrivals permit overlap, but
allocation needs tuning against model shape, batch and peak load. GSLICE’s shadow-instance
load time is a transition cost; its GUE is not a generic GPU-busy percentage [13]. iGniter’s
initial p99 violation before shadow-process recovery qualifies its later operating point [15]. MPS
supports an engineering control for capacity but the extracted experiments do not establish it as
a memory-security or fault-isolation guarantee.

4.3    MIG and combinations
MIG allocates hardware-defined GPU instances on supported devices. It can give explicit
resource partitions, but a slice is finite: model weights, batch memory and number of concurrently
provisioned instances must fit. GNN-Triton reports thrashing beyond the local slice’s model
capacity [20]. Dyna-P shows that more smaller partitions can increase concurrent jobs while
increasing mean JCT [21]; that result does not measure online p99. ParvaGPU, MIGER,
MMK, BOER, FluidFaaS and Protean add MPS, grouping, placement, migration or serverless
orchestration [22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27]. Their whole-system figures include scheduling decisions
in addition to local partition behavior. MIGServe’s four-GPU sustainable rate at 90% SLO
attainment is not a one-GPU p99 measurement [28].
Hybrid packing is conditional. BOER identifies a local case where a ParvaGPU-style hybrid
underperforms MIG-only serving for larger models, while helping a light MobileNet case [25].
EnerTune’s energy-aware placement shows that the most utilized pairing need not minimize
energy, and even its favorable local energy pairing changes latency [29]. MIG partitioning does
not automatically establish that every security or fault-containment property was tested in these
papers. Exact supported profiles, reconfiguration disruption and threat model need to be checked
against the target hardware before a final deployment recommendation.

4.4    API remoting, virtualization and runtime control
An API layer can intercept or route calls, isolate runtime objects, schedule kernels or move
contexts/data across boundaries. Torpor gives a directly measured warning: a BERT example


                                                7
reports native 42 ms, remote 43 ms, PCIe swap 144 ms and NVLink swap 45 ms in its Table 4
[30]. These are the paper’s setting, not universal interface overheads. StreamBox, KRYPTON,
Tally, Orion, XSched, ArkGPU and SGDRC differ substantially in intercepted APIs, process
assumptions, memory behavior and scheduling policy [31, 32, 33, 5, 6, 34, 35]. An API wrapper
changes the trusted implementation surface and can impose transfer, copying, synchronization
or compatibility costs. KRYPTON contains a specific fault-propagation test; one experiment
cannot be generalized to all wrappers [32]. StreamBox’s sandbox separation likewise should not
be promoted to proof of adversarial security [31].


5    Metrics, operating conditions and RQ2–RQ3
Several studies report measured improvements that are deliberately non-comparable across
strata. REEF’s GV100 PyTorch DISB-C experiment compares normalized p99 and separately
normalized throughput against Orion; a TensorRT stratum gives a throughput counterexample
[3]. InferFair’s local Table 3 reports a goodput and slowdown-fairness result under a particular
load ratio, with smaller differences at other ratios [36]. AdMix’s local TP1 and TP2 experiments
use distinct LLM models and device counts; their best-effort throughput ratios are not pooled
[17]. The SoCC interference study measures P90 TBT under synthetic co-tenants and finds that
separated SM sets still share cache and memory-bandwidth pressure [37]. Hardware partitions
and request-level isolation therefore require separate measurements.
Edge GPU studies add useful control ideas but limited direct transfer to data-center MIG/MPS.
Miriam coordinates elastic kernels on edge GPUs lacking the server mechanisms; its paper’s
upper-bound throughput and critical-task overhead are paired outcomes under its evaluated mix
[9]. SPLIT evaluates model-block scheduling and violations on one edge GPU [8]. Edge-sky
couples GPU, CPU and Edge TPU placement, so its combined throughput is not a pure GPU-
sharing effect [38]. WattWiser uses four AMD MI50 devices for placement and power; local
shared pairs inform latency while server power is a wider result [39]. ITIF’s shared transformer
backbone changes computation itself, so its gain is not a scheduler-only comparison [40].
Arrival shape and model composition influence the winner. FaST-GShare’s local eight-pod
RNNT setting differs from a single racing pod; the model, share and tail threshold define its
result [16]. Deft shows that synchronization frequency selected for one batch or image size can
inflate another job’s completion time after a workload change [41]. EnerTune’s power/frequency
and batching settings change the resource objective [29]. FluidFaaS and ESG report workflow
or system SLO effects that combine local GPU sharing with pipeline design and placement
[26, 42]. These reports remain relevant to mechanism deployment, but their totals do not identify
the isolated gain from a partition or quota.

6.1    Trade-offs and conflicting evidence
Priority latency versus background throughput is an observed conflict rather than a rhetorical
possibility. Tally’s 10% idle-time stratum reports normalized background throughput 1.19 versus
1.65 for TGS, even though its policy targets protected inference latency [33]. REEF’s PyTorch
priority result and its separate TensorRT throughput comparison show that the conclusion
changes with runtime and denominator [3]. We infer that a latency-first controller may knowingly
leave capacity or best-effort progress on the table; the size of that sacrifice must be measured at

                                                9
the target arrival rates. This does not establish Tally or REEF as globally better or worse.
Packing versus interference is another genuine conflict. VastPipe reports local pair gains over
default MPS in its A100 configuration [14]; DeInfer observes that MPS limits still permit
shared-resource latency interference [18]. The SoCC Green Context experiments separate SM
sets but not L2 and off-chip bandwidth, with substantial P90 TBT sensitivity under a synthetic
copy or sleep co-tenant [37]. Their models, GPU generations, metrics and intervention layers
differ. We infer that quota-only or SM-only allocation should be accompanied by per-tenant
tail measurements and a memory-traffic stress case, rather than that the papers disagree on one
controlled experiment.
Partition density versus model fit is visible in GNN-Triton’s local thrashing threshold and Dyna-
P’s concurrency/JCT tension [20, 21]. BOER’s larger-model counterexample further prevents
“MIG+MPS always beats MIG” [25]. MIGServe and FluidFaaS evaluate broader server systems,
so their rates and SLO hit percentages cannot refute a one-GPU memory-capacity result [28, 26].
The amount of device memory, model weights, batch, chosen instance layout and migration
policy are plausible effect modifiers, not measured universal coefficients.
Resource utilization versus energy is a separate objective. EnerTune’s local SDXL/MobileNetV3
pairing consumes less energy than no sharing while increasing latency in that example [29].
WattWiser’s aggregate power outcome concerns four AMD GPUs and placement [39]. Neither
demonstrates a universal energy benefit from every MIG profile or from simply increasing GPU
busy time. The objective function should include electricity, SLO penalties and the cost of extra
devices when those matter.


7    Answers to RQ5 and engineering recommendation
For a strict per-tenant latency SLO and a high-priority versus best-effort mix, begin with exclu-
sive admission or a temporal controller whose measured preemption, handover and model-fit
behavior satisfy the target percentile. REEF, Clockwork, Sirius, XSched and related experiments
support the possibility of protecting priority work in their own settings [3, 1, 4, 6]. Reserve
capacity or accept lower background throughput when necessary; Tally supplies a direct caution
[33]. This is an inference from heterogeneous studies, not a certified SLO for a new GPU.
A final target workload trace, percentile and allowed throughput loss are needed to make the
deployment choice concrete.
For density with compatible small models and flexible SLOs, trial MPS with explicit per-model
caps and interference monitoring, using solo and concurrent baselines at realistic burst rates
[12, 13, 14, 18]. Report achieved goodput and tail latency alongside density. If memory footprint
or cache/bandwidth contention dominates, a cap alone is insufficient. If supported hardware and
model fit permit it, trial MIG partitions for more explicit allocation; test several profiles and
the cost of reconfiguration, then compare MIG-only and MIG+MPS on the same service mix
[20, 21, 25]. BOER’s adverse hybrid case is a reason to retain the MIG-only baseline.
Choose API remoting or virtualization when it supplies required fine-grained control or process
integration and measured call, transfer and context overhead fits the SLO. Benchmark the actual
PCIe/NVLink path, tensor sizes and client/runtime compatibility. Torpor illustrates why fabric
selection changes latency [30]. A security or fault-containment requirement additionally calls for
a specified threat model and direct testing; performance-interference control cannot substitute



                                               10
for that evidence. No cross-mechanism adversarial security ranking is justified by the current
extraction.
Use an explicit decision record: target GPU and supported features; peak and burst arrival trace;
model and batch memory; p95/p99 or TTFT/TBT SLO; minimum goodput and tenant count;
allowed reconfiguration interruption; energy or hardware-cost objective; security/fault threat
model; then a same-device A/B comparison with solo and shared baselines. A mechanism is
acceptable only if it meets the hard constraint in that measured setting; choose among acceptable
candidates using the stated secondary objective. The comparison must disclose whether a
whole-server controller, compiler, batching policy or workflow scheduler also changed. This
procedure is our engineering interpretation of the evidence, not a benchmark outcome reported
by one paper.


8    Research gaps and threats to validity
The working extraction shows incomplete apples-to-apples comparisons of time slicing, MPS,
MIG and remoting on one GPU under the same models, arrivals, SLOs, resource budget and
driver/runtime. Tail and fairness metrics are inconsistent. Several systems combine local sharing
with batching or global placement, and some energy totals are whole-server rather than per-
partition. Direct fault-injection and adversarial isolation studies are sparse relative to latency
experiments. Recent LLM decoding introduces TTFT/TBT and memory-bandwidth behavior
that does not map neatly to older image-classifier throughput. These gaps follow from the
recorded comparison fields and negative cases; the final gap inventory is subject to the remaining
full texts and parameter extraction.
Review-level threats include incomplete formal export coverage, the ACM displayed/export
mismatch, ScienceDirect filter ambiguity, an amended cutoff, a single AI reviewer, rule-assisted
title exclusions, 45 unassessed extension reports, 96 unresolved retrieval cases and two pending
eligible reports. The interim four-source counts balance algebraically but not yet at every
canonical report/version row. Published studies favor systems with favorable evaluations;
negative findings and inaccessible work may be underrepresented. Some Markdown conversions
lose figure pixels or interleave columns; we retain labelled prose and tables with locations, do
not interpolate plots, and label untranscribed parameters. Quality totals assess reporting, and a
threshold alone cannot resolve indirectness or missing experiments. A final sensitivity analysis
after screening and human review is required before calling the synthesis complete.


9    Conclusion
Current primary evidence supports conditional engineering selection rather than a universal
winner. Temporal admission and preemption can protect prioritized inference with throughput
or integration costs. Tuned MPS can pack compatible models but retains shared-resource
interference. MIG exposes defined partitions with profile, memory and reconfiguration costs;
hybrid gains depend on model size and placement. API control can add fine-grained scheduling
while transfer and compatibility impose workload-specific overhead. Performance protection,
memory separation, adversarial security and fault containment must be evaluated independently.
The 43-study working set and interim PRISMA map must be reconciled with all pending
retrieval, screening and version decisions before this becomes the final submitted SLR.
